Major project · simulation study

Quantum-safe communication between vehicles

Connected vehicles authenticate each other with elliptic-curve cryptography, which a large quantum computer will break. We tested both proposed replacements, quantum key distribution and post-quantum cryptography. We showed that three recently published authentication schemes for drones and autonomous vehicles [1–3] fail against seven concrete attacks. We then designed and formally verified a post-quantum vehicle handshake and measured what it costs on a crowded road.

The problem

V2V security today will not survive a quantum computer

Vehicles broadcast signed safety messages about ten times a second (position, speed, braking) and set up keys with each other for private exchanges. Today both rest on elliptic-curve cryptography (ECDSA and ECDH, as in IEEE 1609.2 [4]). Shor's algorithm [5] solves the elliptic-curve discrete-log problem on a large quantum computer, so every signature could be forged and every recorded key exchange decrypted.

Two replacements are proposed. Quantum key distribution (QKD) [6] uses physics: measuring a photon disturbs it, so an eavesdropper shows up as errors. Post-quantum cryptography (PQC) uses new mathematics that quantum computers are not known to break: NIST standardised ML-KEM [7] and ML-DSA [8] in 2024. Three recent schemes for drone and autonomous-vehicle authentication, PSRS-D2D [1], PSLAP [2] and P²AS-IoAV [3], each come with a formal security argument (a Real-or-Random proof [9] and Scyther verification [10]). The third already uses a post-quantum KEM, CRYSTALS-Kyber [11]. This project asks which approach actually works for vehicles, and at what cost.

Part 1 · Quantum key distribution

QKD works as theory says, but distance limits it

In BB84 [6], Alice sends single photons in random bases; Bob measures in random bases; they keep the bits where the bases matched and compare a sample. An intercept-resend eavesdropper must guess bases too, and her wrong guesses show up as errors (QBER).

Errors in the kept bits are what give Eve away.

How we got itEach dot is a simulation of 200,000 photons. Each line is the formula Q = e + f/4 − f·e/2 (e = channel noise, f = share intercepted), derived step by step in report §1.3. Largest gap between a dot and its line: – standard errors, over – settings.

Why it mattersEve has to guess each photon's basis. Half her guesses are wrong, and then Bob's bit is a coin flip, so every photon she touches adds a 1-in-4 chance of an error. On a clean channel she is caught once she intercepts – of the photons, and intercepting all of them gives a 25% error rate. Channel noise uses up part of that margin: at – noise she is caught from –. Below the line, privacy amplification shortens the key to remove what she could have learned.

Real error correction reveals about –× the theoretical minimum, so the key runs out at – error rather than at the 11% of the Shor–Preskill bound [15]. That bound is why the abort threshold in the chart above sits at 11%. Across – runs, Alice and Bob never ended up with different keys (– mismatches).

Distance is the real limit

What you seeSecret key per photon sent, against the length of the link. The vertical scale is logarithmic: each gridline down means ten times less key, so a straight line here is an exponential fall. How we got itFibre-like loss of 0.2 dB/km, 20% efficient detectors, a dark-count probability of 10⁻⁵ per pulse and an ideal single-photon source (an optimistic setting); 5 runs per distance. Full runs stop producing key earlier, from – km, because each run also pays a fixed 131-bit verification and security cost that the long-run rate leaves out. Derivation in report §1.7.

Why it mattersThe key rate falls – dB per km, matching the fibre loss of 0.2 dB/km: ten times less key every – km. Then detector noise overwhelms the weak signal, and there is no key at – km. QKD also needs line-of-sight optics between moving cars and an authenticated classical channel, which in practice means pre-shared keys or post-quantum signatures.

Part 2 · Cryptanalysis

Three published schemes, seven successful attacks

The schemes are PSRS-D2D [1] (drone-to-drone, IEEE TMC 2026), PSLAP [2] (autonomous vehicle–drone, Computer Networks 2025) and P²AS-IoAV [3] (vehicle–UAV and UAV–cloud with CRYSTALS-Kyber, IEEE TVT 2026). Each was implemented from the paper's own equations and figures on real P-256 and ML-KEM cryptography. Every attacker gets only what the paper's threat model allows; the victim's true secret is used only to check the result. All – attacks on the three schemes recover it exactly. The last row is a separate demonstration of Shor's algorithm [5] against the elliptic-curve/RSA family, not an attack on any of the three papers.

Scope of each attack (read before quoting a result)
  • PSLAP [2], session linking: the text of §4.3 sends the pseudo-identity PIDAV in clear, so any eavesdropper can link a vehicle's sessions. Fig. 3 masks it as TIDAV = h(X) ⊕ PIDAV; against that variant only the drones, not an outside eavesdropper, can link sessions.
  • P²AS-IoAV [3], past-session decryption: needs both parties' long-term private keys, or the trusted authority's copies of them, since the authority generates every private key. The drone's key alone only de-anonymises the vehicle.
  • P²AS-IoAV [3], forged message 1: the drone accepts a message 1 forged under a registered vehicle's identity (impersonation), but the forger does not learn that session's key.
  • PSRS-D2D [1], master-key extraction: two captured drones' certificates give the authority's secrets, which then also let the attacker impersonate drones that were never captured.

The flaws are in the protocol design, not just the curve: session keys built from public values, no forward secrecy, a static identifier sent in clear, a stored password verifier, and certificates whose algebra leaks the master key after two vehicles are captured. Even with a post-quantum KEM (P²AS-IoAV [3]), the design still fails.

Part 3 · Our protocol

PQ-V2V-AKE: a handshake designed against every one of those attacks

Each design rule answers one of the attacks above. The session key comes only from a fresh ML-KEM-768 [7] secret and the full transcript. Each vehicle holds its own ML-DSA [8] key; the authority only signs short-lived pseudonym certificates and keeps a revocation list. The responder checks the initiator before doing any key work.

Vehicle A
initiator
1. fresh ML-KEM public key, pseudonym certificate, nonce, time, signature over all of it
Vehicle B
responder
2. KEM ciphertext, B's certificate, signature over the transcript, key-confirmation MAC
3. A's key-confirmation MAC → both hold the session key

An optional hybrid mode adds X25519, as the standard hybrid key agreement for TLS 1.3 does [16]. Safety messages are signed separately with Falcon-512 [17] and carry only a certificate digest between full certificates.

Compute is not the problem: a few milliseconds. The cost is size. Post-quantum keys, signatures and certificates make a handshake about 15 KB, against under 1 KB for the classical version of the same protocol.

Does a signed safety message still fit in one radio frame?

What you seeThe size of one signed safety message for each choice of signature, against one 1,400-byte radio frame (dashed line). The "authority" is the certificate issuer: its signature sits inside every vehicle certificate. Colour is the vehicle's signature, as in the network charts below.

How we got itReal signatures over a 200-byte payload, encoded as in our implementation. The full certificate goes out once a second; the other nine messages carry an 8-byte digest of it. Size formulas in report §4.2.

Why it mattersML-DSA-44 overflows a 1,400-byte frame even with the digest. Falcon-512 with the digest fits, so Falcon is the signature for broadcasts. The once-a-second message with the full certificate overflows with every post-quantum choice; the simulation splits it into fragments, which real 802.11p cannot do (see Limits at the bottom of the page).

Part 4 · Network simulation

On a crowded road, the radio channel is the bottleneck

A discrete-event simulation (SimPy [18]): a 2 km highway with three lanes each way, 802.11p radio [19] with real airtime, carrier sensing, collisions (including hidden terminals), retries and fragmentation, and a crypto processor in each vehicle. Crypto costs are measured from the real implementations (liboqs [20]), and a mode that runs the real protocol inside the simulation agrees with them.

The bigger bottleneck: signatures on every safety message

What you seeOne line per signature scheme as more cars each broadcast ten signed safety messages a second; shaded bands are 95% confidence ranges. The buttons choose what is measured.

How we got itSafety messages only, no handshakes. "Delivered" means received and verified within 300 m. Steady state, 5 random seeds per point. The channel load follows a formula up to saturation (report §4.5).

Why it mattersTen signed messages a second per vehicle fill the channel. Falcon-512 starts to saturate it from about 100 vehicles (fully busy near 150) and ML-DSA-44 from about 50, while ECDSA uses less than half of it even at 200. At 100 vehicles only – of Falcon-512 messages reach the cars within 300 m. Quantum-safe broadcasting needs more than a signature swap: signing fewer messages, smaller certificates, or congestion control.

Proofs for PQ-V2V-AKE

In a Real-or-Random model [9] where the attacker controls the radio, can steal long-term keys and can capture vehicles, three theorems reduce the protocol's security to that of ML-KEM-768, ML-DSA-44 and HMAC-SHA-256. Theorem 1 gives mutual authentication with key confirmation. Theorem 2 shows the session key stays secret even if every long-term key, and the authority's key, leaks afterwards (perfect forward secrecy). Theorem 3 covers the hybrid mode. The proofs use no random oracles, so they apply to quantum attackers. They are written by hand; the machine-checked part is the Scyther model above.

Writing the unlinkability argument exposed a flaw that no test had caught. Pseudonym certificates started at each vehicle's enrollment time, so that time, taken modulo the certificate lifetime, linked all of a vehicle's pseudonyms. The authority now issues every certificate on one global time grid.

Conclusions

What this means for vehicles

  • Post-quantum cryptography is deployable now. It is software on the existing on-board unit, its compute cost is a few milliseconds, and our protocol keeps every security property the published designs lacked.
  • QKD behaves exactly as theory predicts, and that is its problem for vehicles. It detects any eavesdropper, but needs line-of-sight optics, loses rate exponentially with distance, and still needs an authenticated classical channel, in practice pre-shared keys or post-quantum signatures.
  • The three published schemes [1–3] fail at the design level. All seven attacks on them succeeded, including two on P²AS-IoAV [3], which already uses a post-quantum KEM. Quantum resistance does not help if the protocol design is broken.
  • The real cost of quantum safety is bandwidth. Bigger keys and signatures, not computation, limit how many vehicles a channel can serve. The broadcast layer needs redesigning, not just new algorithms.

Limits. This is a simulation study: no real vehicles, radios or quantum hardware. Timings come from one laptop and vary by up to 2× between runs (CPU frequency scaling), so treat compute figures as an order of magnitude. Safety messages carry a 200-byte payload, and splitting messages over 1,400 bytes into fragments is hypothetical (real 802.11p cannot). Scyther results mean "no attack found within bounds", not a full proof; the computational proofs are hand-written, not machine-checked, and Theorem 3 (hybrid mode) rests on an explicitly stated assumption about HMAC.
